Most of it doesn't need you.

The trouble is that you can't tell which parts do without reading all of it. So the tax isn't the work — it's the sorting. It runs every morning, it never finishes, and it produces nothing.

That's the job Zelos takes.

Everything in.
Four things out.

Every message and every event crosses the same gate. Nothing has a colour until Zelos has ruled on it, and roughly seven in ten never get one. It starts with your email and your calendar. Technical users can add other tools later (Slack, GitHub and more).

For technical users: everything else it can read

Calendars arrive as a subscribed link, a CalDAV account, or a file on your disk. From Settings it also reads Slack, GitHub, Linear, Todoist, Fireflies meeting recaps, any RSS or Atom feed, a folder that your scripts drop files into, and a WhatsApp chat you exported. Each is a token you mint in your own account or a file you own, and all of them are read-only.

It reads everything.
It gives you four things.

  1. Α

    Now

    At most four items. Ever. A board where everything is urgent tells you nothing, so Zelos is forced to choose — and forced to say why it chose.

  2. Β

    Owed

    Both directions. What you promised someone and haven't done, and what you're still waiting on — including the thread where somebody offered you three dates two weeks ago and you never answered.

  3. Γ

    Today

    Everything with a clock on it, in the order it will bite you. Eight on screen, the rest folded underneath — nothing is ever quietly dropped.

  4. Δ

    Your calendar

    A real week grid, with a short prep note for the next meeting built from your own history with the people who'll be in the room.

And where a reply is obviously owed, it writes one — ready to send, with no blanks to fill in. Nothing is ever sent without you clicking send.

For technical users: run the AI on your own computer

Run it yourself local

  • Ollama:11434
  • LM Studio:1234
  • llama.cpp:8080
  • vLLM:8000
  • LocalAIopenai

Zelos scans these ports on first run and offers whatever is already running. Local models need no key at all, and nothing Zelos reads ever leaves your machine.

Two wire protocols — openai and anthropic — cover every hosted provider and every local runtime, because the protocol is what matters, not the company. Switch whenever you like — your data never moves.

It runs in your house.

There is no Zelos server and no Zelos account. Your mail, your calendar, and everything Zelos concludes about them stay in one folder on your own computer. Your passwords are locked away on that computer too, the same way your Mac or PC keeps its other passwords.

Three things leave your computer, and you chose all three: your email provider, your calendar, and the AI service you picked. Add another tool in Settings and that tool is a fourth — again one you chose. Only the AI is ever told what Zelos read, and if the AI runs on your own computer, nothing it read leaves at all.

If you sign in with Google or Microsoft, Zelos talks to them only while you are signing in. Still no server of ours. The privacy policy is the whole list, in plain words.

What leaves this machine

  • Your email provider you chose it
  • Your calendar you chose it
  • The AI service you chose it — or your own computer
  • Any tool you add you chose it
  • Google or Microsoft sign-in only while signing in

Nothing is ever sent to us. No tracking. No statistics. No crash reports.

The only difference is where the AI runs.

Nothing here grows a line you did not choose. A tool you add in Settings arrives the way mail does — on the left, read-only, from the one place it lives — and the only thing that goes out through the right-hand wall is still that one request to the AI. Nothing is sent to us: no tracking, no statistics, no crash reports, no update checks, nothing fetched from anywhere else.

Nobody else's code. The part of Zelos that reads your mail and talks to the AI is written from scratch and borrows no packages from anyone, so there is nothing hidden inside it to take on trust. It is small enough to read in an afternoon, and anyone can.

One thing said plainly: your mail is written by other people, so a message can contain text aimed at the AI. Zelos never acts on what the AI says — it shows you, and you click. That is a stronger promise than claiming an AI can't be fooled.

For the technically minded

Your keys live in your operating system's keychain — macOS Keychain, Windows DPAPI, Linux libsecret. On a machine with none of those they go to an encrypted file in the Zelos home, and the app says so.

Every outbound host is named by the connector that reads it, and the one transport they all share refuses any other. Signing in with Google or Microsoft talks to that provider's sign-in service only while you sign in and while it refreshes the token.

The payload list is the same in both columns because there is no local branch in the prompt builder: one cap — 4,000 characters per body inside a fixed 32,000-character budget — runs for a hosted model and a local one alike, and a crowded sweep shrinks it for both. Turning off sendBodies drops each body to the stored 240-character snippet.

Zero dependencies. Not "few" — zero. Zelos installs no packages: no supply chain to trust, no postinstall script, nothing to audit but the code itself. One qualifier, and it is the only one: the two desktop apps bundle Electron — Chromium and Node — because that is what gives them a window and a tray icon. It sits in the shell, not in the program; the code that reads your mail and talks to a model is the same dependency-free code either way, and the source download has no Electron in it at all. That is what makes the claim checkable rather than merely stated — point lsof -i at it and count the conversations against your own settings.

      Why does this happen?
      
      The Mac build's ad-hoc signature checks the app's internal consistency but does not
        verify who made it. Windows has no publisher signature on these installers. Neither a
        warning nor its absence guarantees that an app is safe. Published SHA-256 checksums let
        you compare your download with this release's files; they are not an independent security review.
      
      
      Two more things worth knowing. The Apple silicon build is single-architecture: an Intel
        Mac refuses it outright with a different message that has no Open Anyway in it,
        and Rosetta does not translate in that direction — take the Intel build from the plate
        above. And on macOS and Linux, Zelos locks its own files down to your account; Windows has
        no equivalent to set, so there the protection is the one your user profile already carries
        — which means an administrator on that machine can read it.

    For experts: running from source
    
      The zip is the whole program, and it is small enough to read. No bundled
      runtime, no packages, nothing to install. Unzip it, then run node zelos.mjs —
      Node 22.16 or newer, or 24 or newer. Not simply "22 or newer": Zelos
      searches with the SQLite that comes built into Node, and that build has no full-text search
      before 22.16 or anywhere in the Node 23 line. On one of those, Zelos says so and stops rather
      than half-starting. Then it opens in your browser at 127.0.0.1 and stays
      there.
      
    
      The two apps above are a different trade: around 100 MB, and Electron. The
      same Zelos core, wrapped in a Chromium window so that it has a tray icon and does not live in
      a browser tab — 97 to 126 MB to download depending on which one you take, and roughly 300 MB
      once it is unpacked on disk. The Windows plates are installers: they ask where to put it,
      then add a Start-menu entry and a desktop shortcut. The Mac plates are disk images you drag
      across. If a supply chain is the thing you are weighing, take the zip — it is the one with
      nothing in it but Zelos.
